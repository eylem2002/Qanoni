Let's dig into Contract Drafting — this is the core of your whole product, so it's worth getting precise about how it actually works end to end.

**The user-facing flow**

A lawyer opens WhatsApp (or the web app), picks a contract type — employment, rental, service, NDA, or supplier agreement — and the system asks five questions in plain Arabic, one at a time, conversational style rather than a rigid form. For an employment contract, that might look like: who are the two parties (names, national ID or company registration), what's the job title and start date, what's the salary and payment schedule, what's the contract duration and probation period, and are there any special terms (non-compete, remote work, benefits). The lawyer answers in natural Arabic — "Ahmad Khalil, starts next Monday, 600 dinars a month" — and doesn't need to know legal terminology or formatting conventions; the system handles structuring that into something legally precise.

**What happens underneath those five questions**

This is where the real engineering is, and it's also where you differ most sharply from "just use ChatGPT." The five answers don't get fed raw into a generic prompt that says "write me a contract." Instead there's a pipeline: first, the answers get mapped onto a structured data object (party names, dates, amounts, contract subtype) rather than staying as loose text — this is what makes the document machine-checkable later. Second, that structured data gets merged into a pre-vetted Jordanian legal template for that contract type — a template that a real lawyer has already reviewed and that already contains the mandatory clauses required under Jordanian labor law (Article references, end-of-service indemnity language, notice periods, etc.) so the AI isn't inventing legal boilerplate from scratch every time, it's filling in a structure that's already correct. Third, the LLM's job is narrower and safer than "draft a legal contract" — it's "populate this verified template with this data, in fluent formal Arabic, and flag anything contradictory or missing." That's a meaningfully different (and much lower-risk) task than open-ended generation, and it's also why grounding in actual Jordanian law codes matters: the templates and clause library should be built directly from legislation.jo text plus whatever you can get from the University of Petra dataset, not from the model's general training knowledge of "contracts" which skews heavily toward US/UK law.

**The legal-grounding layer**

Before anything is shown to the lawyer, the draft should pass through a verification pass — checking salary against minimum wage law, checking notice periods against the Jordanian Labor Law minimums, checking that mandatory clauses (end-of-service benefits, working hours, termination conditions) are present. This is essentially a smaller, deterministic rules-check layered on top of the generative step, and it's the thing that turns "an AI wrote a contract" into "an AI wrote a contract that won't get a lawyer in trouble." This is also your strongest differentiation point versus a lawyer just prompting ChatGPT directly, since ChatGPT has no persistent, verified template library or rules layer — it's drafting from general knowledge every single time with no consistency guarantee.

**Output and refinement**

Once generated, the contract renders as a formatted document — proper Arabic typography (right-to-left, correct numeral formatting, professional layout), the firm's logo if branding is set up, and it's exportable as PDF or Word, plus a one-tap "send via WhatsApp" action. If the lawyer wants changes, they type something like "make the probation period three months instead of two" in plain Arabic, and the system patches just that field in the structured data and regenerates the affected section — it doesn't regenerate the whole document from scratch, which keeps edits fast and avoids accidentally changing unrelated clauses.

**Where this differs from Harvey's approach**

Harvey's drafting is research-grounded — it pulls from precedent, prior firm documents, and case law because its users are doing complex M&A or litigation work where there's no single "correct" template, just judgment calls informed by precedent. Your problem is the opposite and, frankly, easier: Jordanian employment, rental, and NDA contracts are highly standardized, so a verified-template-plus-structured-data approach is more reliable than precedent-based generation would be, and it's cheaper to build and easier to guarantee correctness on. The tradeoff is that this approach won't scale well to genuinely novel contract types or complex negotiated agreements — but that's fine, because that's not your market.
